

Chapter 5 – The Last Tuesday


The room was full of people.

Relatives Vivin hadn't seen in years stood against the walls — his wife's brother from Nagercoil, his own cousin from Madurai, faces he recognized but couldn't immediately name. They stood in clusters, speaking in whispers, some with phones pressed to their ears, some staring at the floor.

Meera sat on the edge of the bed. Her face was swollen. Her hair was undone — she never let her hair down in front of people, never — and her hands lay in her lap, palms up, empty, like she had been holding something that had been taken from her.

Aadhav sat beside her. Not the loud, wild, biscuit-measuring boy. A small, silent shape pressed against his mother's side, his face buried in her arm. His shoulders shook, but he made no sound.

And in the centre of the room — on the floor, on a mat, covered in a white cloth — was a shape.

A shape the size of a man.

Vivin's legs stopped working.

---

He stood in the doorway. He could not move. He could not speak. He could not look away.

The white cloth covered everything except the face. The face was turned slightly to the side — the way Vivin turned his head when he slept. The eyes were closed. The mouth was a straight, still line.

He knew the face.

He knew the mole above the left eyebrow. He knew the scar on the chin from a lathe accident fifteen years ago. He knew the shape of the jaw, the width of the forehead, the way the hair fell across the temple in a way that no amount of combing could fix.

It was his face.

It was him.

Lying on the floor of his own bedroom, surrounded by his own family, covered in white.

---

The sound came from somewhere deep inside him — not his throat, not his chest. From a place he didn't know existed. A sound that wasn't a word and wasn't a scream. Something older than language. The sound a man makes when his mind refuses what his eyes are showing him.

He moved forward. The people in the room didn't part. They didn't need to — he moved through them the way light moves through glass. Without resistance. Without contact. Without being seen.

"Meera."

She didn't look up.

"Meera, I'm here. I'm right here."

She drew in a shaking breath and said something to the woman beside her — something about the shop, about Selvam, about who would call the people who hadn't been called yet. Her voice was flat. Mechanical. The voice of a woman doing the things that need to be done because stopping would mean feeling what she wasn't ready to feel.

Vivin dropped to his knees beside her. He reached for her hand.

His fingers passed through.

Not around. Not over. Through. Like his hand was made of smoke.

He reached for Aadhav. The boy was trembling, his face still hidden. Vivin tried to touch his hair — that stubborn, uncombed hair that stood up in the morning like a protest against gravity.

His hand went through.

Nothing. He was nothing.

---

He stood up.

The room continued around him. The whispered conversations. The ringing phones. The soft clinking of tea glasses being prepared in the kitchen. The sounds of a house that was doing what houses do when someone has died — filling itself with people and noise to push back against the silence that will come later, when everyone leaves and the family is alone.

Vivin walked out of the room.

He moved through the hall. Past the kitchen, where a neighbour was boiling water. Past the front door, where his wife's brother stood smoking, his face hard and tired. Past the courtyard, where Aadhav's untied shoes still lay by the door.

He stepped outside.

The morning light was soft. The street was busier now — a few auto-rickshaws had started their rounds, and the tea shop near the corner had opened its shutters. The milkman's bicycle bell rang somewhere far away.

The dogs were still at the gate. Lying down now, chins on paws, waiting for biscuits that weren't coming.

---

And that's when he saw them.

Three figures, standing in the narrow lane beside his house.

Vivin stopped.

A boy stood in the centre. Maybe five, maybe six — the same child from the beach, the one who had stood at the water's edge the first time, the one who had turned to look at him in the instant before everything vanished. The boy held a bottle of Appy Fizz in a beer glass, gripping it with both hands the way children hold things they're afraid to drop.

To his left stood a teenager. Fourteen, fifteen — the sharp jaw, the untucked white shirt, the eyes that had been waiting for years. The boy from the bar, the one who had said I've been looking for you, the one who had laughed when the bartender served the fizz.

And to his right stood a man. Tall, calm, rolled sleeves. The man from the café, the one who had tossed him the keys, the one who had sat beside him for what felt like years on a road that led nowhere. Between his fingers, he turned something over and over — a small brass keychain, shaped like a lathe chisel, its edges worn smooth.

Three figures. Three ages.

All of them looking at him.

All of them with his face.

---

Vivin's eyes went red before his mind caught up with why.

The tears came first — fast, hot, soundless. They fell from a place that had nothing to do with understanding and everything to do with knowing. His body had figured it out before his brain had — the way the body always does. The way your stomach drops a full second before your mind registers the fall.

He looked at the boy, and understood.

The child on the beach — the one he had met for five seconds on that first Tuesday — was him. Him at five years old, standing on a shore he didn't recognize, waiting for the rest of himself to arrive. The Appy Fizz wasn't a guess. It was a memory. His memory. The drink his mother used to pour for him in a tall glass because he liked pretending it was beer.

He looked at the teenager, and understood.

"I've been looking for you for years." Of course he had. Of course a boy at fifteen — a boy who worked hard and stayed quiet and carried himself like he had already decided what he wanted — would spend years looking for the man he was going to become. And when that man finally arrived, the boy had laughed with relief, not surprise.

He looked at the man, and understood.

The dream car. The keychain. The long, perfect conversation that felt like talking to someone who knew everything about you. It was him. All of him. His prime. His best self. The version of him that had been driving through that nameless world, waiting for the old man to finally show up and sit beside him.

The beach wasn't another world.

It was the place where every version of Vivin waited.

He hadn't been visiting them.

He had been arriving.

---

Vivin stood in the lane outside his house and looked at the three figures — the boy, the teenager, the man — and they looked back at him. Nobody spoke. There was nothing left to say.

Down the lane, through the open front door, he could hear Meera crying.

He could hear Aadhav's silence — that terrible, too-big silence of a child who has just learned a word he will never be able to unlearn.

He could hear the phone ringing. He could hear his cousin's voice answering, saying yes, yes, this morning, no, they don't know exactly when — sometime in the night, Selvam found him at the shop —

Selvam found him at the shop.

Vivin closed his eyes.

---

He had felt it. That first Tuesday. The uneasiness, the snap, the five seconds on the beach. That wasn't a vision. That wasn't stress. That was a mild heart attack — the kind that passes without leaving much evidence, the kind a man ignores because he has orders to fill and a family to feed and who has time to sit in a doctor's office when there's a coupling sleeve due on Thursday?

He had told no one.

He had gone to work the next Tuesday like nothing had happened. He had stood at his lathe and run the machine and fed the steel and done the good, honest work he had always done.

And the second heart attack — the one that came while his hands were on the machine, while the coupling sleeve was half-finished, while the fluorescent tube buzzed above him — had been the one that didn't pass.

The lathe was still spinning when Selvam found him on the floor in the morning.

---

The child took a sip from his glass.

The teenager turned and looked at the water that only they could see.

The man turned the keychain over between his fingers, one last time, and slipped it into his pocket.

Vivin looked at his own hands. He turned them over, slowly. Palms up.

The lines were gone. No creases. No calluses from twenty-five years of lathe work. No scar across the left thumb from the accident that had taught him to respect the machine. The fingerprints — those spirals that had been pressed into every contract, every bank form, every smudged glass of Appy Fizz — were smooth. Blank. Like skin that had never touched anything.

Like the boy's hands on the beach.

Like the teenager's.

Like the man's.

He understood now.

---

The dogs at the gate lifted their heads as if they'd heard something — a frequency too high for the street to notice. They whined softly, then lowered their heads again.

Inside the house, Meera pressed her face into Aadhav's hair and held him the way you hold something you're afraid the world will take next.

Karthik arrived at the front gate, out of breath, his face white. He stood at the doorway for a long moment, unable to enter, unable to leave. Then he went in.

Selvam sat on the workshop floor, alone, next to the machine that was still warm. He held a brass keychain in his hands — the one he had made twelve years ago, the one he thought had been lost. He had found it in Vivin's apron pocket that morning, pressed against the old man's chest like a coin saved for a journey.

---

Outside, in the narrow lane, the four figures stood together.

A child.

A boy.

A man.

And Vivin — who was all of them, who had always been all of them, standing at the end of a street he had walked down ten thousand times, looking at his own home for the last time.

The light shifted. Not sunrise — there would be no sunrise where they were going.

Just that same soft, even, nameless light.

The one from the beach.

The one that had no clock.

Vivin turned away from the house. He looked at the boy, who smiled. He looked at the teenager, who nodded. He looked at the man, who simply waited.

He took a step.

Then another.

And the street — his street, with its cracked pavement and its stray dogs and its jasmine plants and its sleeping sons — grew quiet behind him.

The way streets do when someone who belonged to them has finally gone.
