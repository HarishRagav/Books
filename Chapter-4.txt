

Chapter 4 – The Morning After


The street was beautiful.

Vivin hadn't noticed that before — not like this, not the way he noticed it now. The pale light before sunrise had turned the junction into something soft. The road was empty. The shutters were down. A single streetlight buzzed at the corner, throwing a circle of orange onto the asphalt.

He slowed from a run to a walk, catching his breath, grinning like an idiot.

He was home. This was his street. These were his shops, his junction, his town. The same cracked pavement. The same auto-rickshaws parked in a crooked row. The same stray cats sitting on the wall near the tailor's shop, watching nothing.

Everything was exactly where he had left it.

---

He turned the corner onto his street and saw the baby.

Every morning, a young mother sat on the steps of the corner house with her infant on her lap. Every morning, Vivin waved. Every morning, the baby stared at him with those large, confused eyes that babies have — not recognizing him, but tracking the movement, the way babies track anything that moves.

The mother was there now, sitting on the step, the baby in her arms.

Vivin waved. Big, happy wave. The kind of wave you give when you're so glad to be alive that even strangers deserve to know about it.

The baby looked in his direction.

But the baby's eyes didn't land on him. They drifted — past him, through him — and settled on something behind him. The baby's mouth opened in that wide, gummy, delighted shape babies make when they see something familiar.

Vivin didn't notice. He was already walking past, still smiling, still breathing in the morning air like it was the first air he'd ever tasted.

---

Halfway down the street, the dogs came.

Three of them — the same three that waited for him every morning near his gate. He fed them biscuits from a packet he kept in his shop. They knew the sound of his footsteps. They knew the rhythm of his walk. Every morning, the moment he turned the corner, they came bounding toward him, tails wild, mouths open, the pure stupid joy of dogs who have learned to expect kindness.

They came now.

Vivin saw them and laughed. "Come on, come on — I don't have anything today, but I'll get you something, okay? I'll —"

The dogs ran past him.

Not around him. Not near him. Past him. Through the space where he stood, as if it were empty, and continued running toward the gate behind him, tails wagging, circling and sniffing at the ground near the gate's bottom hinge, where Vivin usually stood when he fished the biscuits out of his pocket.

Vivin stopped.

He turned and looked at the dogs. They were sniffing the gate, whining softly, pushing their noses under the gap. Looking for the thing they expected. Finding nothing.

"Hey," Vivin said. "I'm right here."

The dogs didn't look up.

A cold finger touched the base of his spine. Quick. Light. Gone before he could name it.

He shook his head. "They already ate," he muttered. "Selvam must have fed them." He laughed again, but this laugh came out thin. Flat. Like a photocopy of the last one.

He turned back toward his house.

---

The back gate was open.

Vivin usually entered through the back — old habit. The front door faced the street, and he never liked the sound of the lock echoing at 3 AM. The back gate opened into a narrow courtyard, and from there, a door led directly into the hall.

He stepped through the gate. The courtyard was quiet. Meera's jasmine plant sat in its pot by the wall. The plastic bucket she used for the kolam was turned upside down on the step. A pair of Aadhav's school shoes lay near the door, laces untied, exactly the way the boy always left them.

Home.

He pushed open the back door and stepped into the hall.

The hall was dark. Not dark like nighttime — the kind of dark that happens when people are inside but haven't opened the curtains. A thick, airless dark. The air smelled different. Not the usual morning smell of coffee and idli batter. Something heavier. Something he couldn't place.

He could hear sounds coming from the bedroom.

Voices. Low, murmuring voices. Not one or two — several. The kind of sound a room makes when it's full of people who are trying to speak softly.

And under the voices, another sound.

Crying.

Not loud. Not dramatic. The kind of crying that comes from deep inside a person's chest, the kind that has no energy left for volume. Small, broken sounds. One voice — Meera's voice — repeating something over and over, too soft for him to catch the words.

Vivin's smile vanished.

Something was wrong. Something was wrong with Aadhav, or with Meera, or with someone — why were there so many people in his house at this hour? Why was his wife crying? Why —

He took a step forward.

"Meera?"

No response.

He took another step. The hall floor was cold under his bare feet — when had he lost his shoes? He couldn't remember. It didn't matter. He moved toward the bedroom door.

"Meera! What happened? Is Aadhav —"

The voices continued. Nobody turned. Nobody answered.

Vivin reached the doorway.

He looked inside.

And stopped.
